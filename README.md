# AI and the Future of U.S. Work

**Exploring AI Occupational Exposure, Wages, and Projected Employment Growth (2024–2034)**

*Data Analytics Capstone Project | Correlation One*

## Project Overview

Advances in artificial intelligence and automation are rapidly changing the U.S. labor market and creating uncertainty about future employment opportunities. Workers, educators, employers, and policymakers need reliable information to identify which occupations are projected to grow, which may be more vulnerable to technological disruption, and where workforce opportunities may emerge.

This project examines relationships between AI Occupational Exposure (AIOE), wages, education requirements, and projected U.S. employment growth from 2024 to 2034. By combining occupational data from the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics with AIOE measures, the analysis explores how exposure to AI varies across occupations and what these patterns may mean for career resilience.

## Research Questions

1. Which U.S. occupational categories exhibit the greatest exposure to artificial intelligence?
2. What relationships exist between AI exposure, occupational earnings, education requirements, and projected employment growth?
3. How can workers and stakeholders use these indicators to identify potentially resilient or vulnerable career paths?

## Tools & Methodology

**Tools:** Microsoft Excel, Tableau Public

**Analytical techniques:** Data cleaning, dataset integration, SOC-code matching, missing-value imputation, exploratory data analysis, correlation analysis, and data visualization.

The project combines U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics employment projections with AI Occupational Exposure data. Missing AIOE values were imputed using averages from corresponding four-digit or broader two-digit SOC occupational groups.

## Key Findings

- **AI exposure and job growth:** A weak negative correlation (approximately −0.25) suggests that higher AI exposure is not strongly associated with lower projected employment growth.
- **High-growth occupations:** Computer and Mathematical occupations demonstrated approximately 9.6% projected growth, despite the relevance of AI to many knowledge-based tasks.
- **Wages and exposure:** Occupations earning over $100,000 had the highest average AIOE (0.75) and strongest average projected growth (5.42%) among the wage groups analyzed.
- **Education requirements:** Occupations typically requiring bachelor's and graduate degrees exhibited relatively high AI exposure.
- **Occupational differences:** Office and Administrative Support and Production occupations showed projected employment declines of approximately 6.4% and 5.0%, respectively.

**Key takeaway:** Higher AI exposure does not necessarily indicate future job elimination. Evaluating exposure alongside earnings, employment projections, education requirements, and occupational characteristics provides a more complete picture of potential workforce vulnerability.

## Business Impact

This analysis offers a framework for evaluating workforce trends and identifying areas where technological change may affect occupational tasks and employment opportunities.

Employers and workforce development organizations can use these insights to inform reskilling priorities, talent planning, and training investments. Individuals can use the findings to evaluate career paths using multiple labor-market indicators rather than relying solely on perceived automation risk.

## Limitations

AI Occupational Exposure measures potential overlap between occupational tasks and AI capabilities. It does not directly measure the probability of job displacement. Employment projections are estimates, and observed relationships should not be interpreted as evidence that AI exposure causes changes in employment.
